Salted sidewalks often show up before the real snow does. Your dog comes back from a walk limping for a few steps, or spends the evening licking their paws. The culprit is usually the same: the salt and ice melt spread just about everywhere.

The good news is that it's mostly irritation, and serious poisoning is rare. In most cases, a few simple habits after each walk are enough to keep their paw pads in good shape all winter.

What salt does to paw pads

Sidewalks and driveways are treated with several kinds of products. The most common is sodium chloride, classic rock salt. There are also ice melts made with calcium, potassium or magnesium chloride. According to the ASPCA, rock salt can irritate and damage paws, while the other chlorides are harder on the digestive tract if swallowed.

The trouble mostly comes from repeated contact. Mixed with snow and ice, salt and grit stick to paws. They can cause irritation, cracks and even chemical burns, says Dr. Valérie Bissonnette, president of the Association des médecins vétérinaires du Québec (AMVQ).

Crystals also lodge between the toes, where fur traps snow. A dog with long hair between the pads often comes home with little balls of salty ice that rub with every step.

When your dog licks their paws: the real risk

Dogs rarely eat ice melt on purpose. They usually swallow it while cleaning their paws after a walk, or by eating dirty snow along the curb. Most of the time the amount is small and the result is nothing more than an upset stomach.

Salt poisoning is possible, but it takes a fair amount. The risk goes up sharply if your dog gets into a spilled bag of ice melt in the garage or entryway. Large amounts of rock salt can push blood sodium to dangerous levels. Calcium- or magnesium-based products can cause vomiting or diarrhea, sometimes with blood.

⚠️ A far more serious winter hazard than salt: antifreeze. Its sweet taste attracts animals, and just a few drops can cause severe kidney damage. Wipe up any puddle in the garage right away and store containers out of reach.

Signs to watch for

Dogs rarely complain about sore paws. Their behaviour and the look of their pads tell you the most.

What you noticeWhat to do
Lifts a paw or limps for a few steps on a salted sidewalk, then carries on normally🟢 Common reaction: rinse and dry their paws when you get home
Dry, rough or slightly red pads, more licking than usual🟡 Keep an eye on it: after-walk routine, balm, protection for the next outings
Cracks, a wound, limping that doesn't go away, nonstop licking, spreading redness🟠 Vet advice: call your clinic
Ate ice melt (spilled bag), repeated vomiting, trembling, seems disoriented or very lethargic🔴 Emergency: contact a veterinary clinic right away

After a dog swallows a de-icing product, vomiting is often the first sign. Others can follow: diarrhea, lethargy, tremors, trouble breathing, disorientation and even seizures. According to the American College of Veterinary Pharmacists, these signs can appear within three hours. Don't wait to see how things go, and don't try to make your dog vomit without a vet's advice. The after-walk routine

This is the habit that makes the biggest difference, and it takes less than two minutes. The goal is to remove the salt before your dog does it with their tongue.

  • Rinse paws with lukewarm water, never hot. A bowl by the door or a damp cloth is enough. Salt dissolves in water, so there's no need for soap.
  • Dry well between the toes and pads. Leftover moisture leads to cracking. Wipe the belly and lower legs too, where slushy snow builds up.
  • Remove ice balls stuck in the fur instead of pulling on them.
  • Take a quick look at the pads: redness, cracks or a small cut from sharp ice.

Set up a small station by the door with everything you need. A microfibre towel absorbs a lot of water and dries fast, which helps when walks pile up.

Boots or balm: which protection to choose?

The two work together more than they compete. The right choice depends mostly on your dog and the kind of outings you take.

Boots, the most complete protection

When a dog accepts them, boots are still the best protection against salt, sharp ice and cold. They're well suited to city walks on heavily salted sidewalks. You do need the right size, though, and some patience while your dog gets used to them.

  • Measure the width of the paw while it's on the ground, bearing weight, and follow the maker's size chart.
  • Have your dog wear them indoors for a few minutes, with treats, before the first outing.
  • Expect a funny walk at first: it's normal and usually fades after a few outings.
  • After each walk, check that they aren't rubbing and that no snow got inside.

Protective wax, before you head out

If your dog flatly refuses boots, a protective wax or cream applied to the pads before going out creates a barrier against salt and dryness. It protects less than a boot, but it helps a lot on short trips. Apply a thin layer and let it soak in for a few minutes.

Moisturizing balm, after the walk

Once the paws are rinsed and dried, a balm made for dogs helps dry or cracked pads stay supple. Choose a veterinary product or one formulated for pets, since your dog will end up licking some of it. Avoid creams made for people, which can contain ingredients that are irritating or toxic when swallowed.

At home: choosing a gentler ice melt

You can't control what the city or your neighbours put down, but your own driveway is a different story. It's often where your dog spends the most time sniffing.

  • Shovel and scrape first, then use just enough product to loosen the ice. CAA-Québec also recommends combining ice melt with an abrasive to cut down on salt.
  • Use sand or grit for traction where your dog walks most.
  • Read the label. There's no specific standard behind the term "pet safe," the ASPCA points out. These products often contain urea or magnesium chloride, which are gentler, but they can still upset the stomach if swallowed.
  • Keep the bag closed and up high, out of reach, in the garage and the shed alike.

Frequently asked questions about road salt and dogs

Can my dog eat snow?

A little clean snow in the yard is generally not a problem. Avoid grey or slushy snow along streets and sidewalks, though, which can contain salt, ice melt or other products. If your dog eats a lot of it and starts vomiting, see your vet.

What should I do if my dog ate ice melt?

If they only licked their paws after a walk, rinse them and keep an eye on your dog. If they ate the product directly, from a spilled bag for instance, call your clinic or an emergency vet without waiting, and have the package on hand.

Are outdoor cats affected too?

Yes. A cat that goes outside walks on the same surfaces and cleans its paws even more thoroughly than a dog. The same precautions apply: watch its paw pads and wipe its paws when it comes in if needed. Cats are also much more sensitive to antifreeze.

Can salt cause frostbite?

Salt doesn't cause frostbite, but irritated, damp pads handle the cold less well. In extreme cold, around −20 °C, take extra care with ears, tail and paw pads. Shorter, more frequent outings are the better option.

A winter without sore paws

You don't need to avoid every salted sidewalk. A rinse after the walk, well-dried paws and protection that suits your dog solve most problems. Keep an eye on their pads, and call your vet as soon as you notice a wound, lasting limp or unusual illness. To spot other warning signs, read our article on signs your dog isn't healthy.
