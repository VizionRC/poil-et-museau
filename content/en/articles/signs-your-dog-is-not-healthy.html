🐾 Our dogs don't talk… but they still communicate.

A change in behaviour, a drop in energy or an unusual detail can be important signals.

Learning to spot them lets you act sooner, before discomfort sets in.

Here are the signs to watch for to know if your dog isn't at its best.

1. Behaviour

The first indicator isn't always physical… it's often behavioural.

You know your dog better than anyone.
If its usual attitude changes, keep an eye on it.

Withdrawal

Your dog usually follows you everywhere… and suddenly prefers to be alone?
This withdrawal can be a way of coping with discomfort or pain.

Irritability

A dog that growls or avoids being petted, especially in certain spots, may simply be trying to tell you it hurts.

Before assuming a behaviour problem, check for any sensitivity.

Lower energy

A dog that's less interested in its games or walks is sending a message.

It's not always serious… but it's never trivial.

💡 Museau's tip: write down changes, even small ones.
It can really help your veterinarian understand the situation.

2. Appetite and hydration

The food bowl often says a lot about how your dog is doing.

Eating less… or not at all

A dog that refuses to eat, especially one that's usually a big eater, deserves your attention.

Drinking much more than usual

If you're refilling the water bowl much more often for no obvious reason, keep an eye on it.

Eating differently

A dog that wolfs down its food or, on the contrary, hesitates to eat may be feeling discomfort.

💡 Well-being tip: a slow feeder bowl can help slow down meals and make it easier to observe its behaviour.

3. Coat and skin

The coat is often a good indicator of health.

A healthy dog usually has a soft, shiny coat.
If it becomes dull, dry or patchy, it may reflect an imbalance.

Watch for:

  • Dry or dull coat
  • Bald patches or redness
  • Frequent licking

A dog that licks a lot is sometimes trying to relieve discomfort… or stress. A lick mat can also help soothe and calm it.

💡 Keep in mind: repetitive licking isn't always harmless.

4. Mobility and posture

A dog may slow down with age… but some signs deserve your attention.

It seems stiff or hesitates to get up

If it takes longer to get up or seems sore, it may be feeling discomfort.

Unusual posture

The “prayer position” (front of the body on the floor, rear end up) can indicate abdominal pain.

A different tail

A tail held low or moving less than usual can signal discomfort.

💡 Museau's tip: a comfortable, suitable bed can improve rest, especially for more sensitive dogs.

5. Oral hygiene

Bad breath isn't normal.

Persistent bad breath

It can be linked to a dental problem… or reveal something else.

The gums

Look at their colour:

  • Pink: normal
  • Red: irritation
  • Pale: keep an eye on it
  • Bluish: emergency

6. Breathing and vital signs

A change in breathing should get your attention.

Panting at rest

If your dog pants for no reason (no heat or exercise), it may indicate discomfort.

Persistent cough

A cough that lasts several days deserves a veterinary opinion.

7. Emergency signs

Sometimes you shouldn't wait.

🚨 See a vet quickly if your dog has:

  • A swollen, hard belly
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Seizures
  • Inability to urinate
  • Blood in vomit or urine

Your dog counts on you to understand what it can't say.

Noticing changes, even subtle ones, can make all the difference.

🐾 And if something seems unusual… trust yourself.

FAQ

Does my dog have a fever?

Without a thermometer, some signs can alert you:
very warm ears, fatigue, red gums.

Why is my dog shaking?

Cold, stress… or sometimes pain.
Look at the context.

Why does my dog eat grass?

It's often normal.
But if it happens often along with vomiting, keep an eye on it.

Is it normal that my dog sleeps a lot?

Yes, it's normal.
But a sudden change should alert you.

According to veterinarians at VCA Animal Hospitals and the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), changes in appetite, energy, behaviour or physical appearance can sometimes be the first signs that a dog needs a veterinary assessment.
